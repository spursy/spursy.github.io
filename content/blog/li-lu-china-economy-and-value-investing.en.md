On October 5, 2026, Li Lu gave a talk at Columbia Business School. Beyond investing and the AI industry, he offered a remarkably sharp summary of China's economic development model over the past few decades. Twitter user [Sea (@Sea_Bitcoin)](https://x.com/Sea_Bitcoin) distilled his remarks into two threads — one on the macro development model, one on the principles of value investing. This post brings both together.

> This is a paraphrase and synthesis of a third party's distillation of the talk, not Li Lu's verbatim words. Treat the specific figures as approximate, subject to public sources.

## I. China's "Goals and Costs" Model

Li Lu's central observation: looking back over China's past 50 years, roughly **every 10 years or so a new set of national goals is set, resources are concentrated to achieve them, and certain industries and groups bear a heavy cost** along the way.

And the subtle part: **the goal of one decade often becomes the cost of the next.**

### 1. The Industrialization Era: the Environment Pays

China achieved economic takeoff through large-scale industrialization — but the environment, air quality, and rivers all became the cost of that growth.

In the decade that followed, environmental protection became the goal to improve instead: green technology advanced, while polluting enterprises became the ones paying the price.

### 2. The Urbanization Era: Real Estate Becomes the National Goal

Property developers were a key force in achieving this goal. They drove China's urbanization, built the China of today, gave a large share of residents their own homes, and formed the wealth base of middle-class families.

But over the past 5–10 years, the real estate industry turned into the side paying the cost.

### 3. 2015–2025: Manufacturing Upgrade Becomes the National Goal

In 2015, China faced the question of how to cross the "middle-income trap." It responded with the ten-year plan *Made in China 2025*, a deliberate push to move manufacturing from the low-to-mid end toward the mid-to-high end.

Along the way came the Trump administration's trade war and the Biden administration's further technology restrictions — which, if anything, strengthened China's resolve to pursue industrial upgrading and technological self-sufficiency. Around 2021, China doubled down, focusing on breaking through bottlenecks in semiconductors, aircraft engines, operating systems, and more, adding "technological self-sufficiency" as a new goal.

In Li Lu's view, a decade on, Chinese manufacturing has steadily moved from the low-to-mid end into the mid-to-high end, and *Made in China 2025* has largely achieved its goals: China is now a global leader in new energy, EVs, batteries, energy storage, and heavy machinery, with major breakthroughs on bottlenecks like semiconductors.

### 4. This Time, Who Pays the Cost?

The answer: **real estate and middle-class household wealth.**

- Property prices peaked in 2021. Over the roughly five years since, they have fallen about **40%** on average, and the total wealth of a typical middle-class family has declined by roughly **60%–70%**.
- Households have responded by cutting consumption, saving more, and paying down debt where they can — leaving domestic consumer demand weak and confidence low.
- Meanwhile, China's annual new-home sales have dropped by **70%**. The roughly **10 trillion yuan** of lost sales happens to equal the amount being poured each year into manufacturing upgrades and efficiency improvements.

That shift in where capital flows is exactly what happened around 2021.

### 5. Constantly Switching Between National Goals and the Costs of Transition

Over the past decades, China has repeatedly set new development goals and borne enormous costs to achieve them; and at times, the national goal of one decade becomes the target that pays the cost in the next.

It is precisely this model that compressed into roughly 40 years a development process other countries might have needed centuries to complete. But for the entrepreneurs, investors, and individuals living through it, the transition can mean enormous pain.

Li Lu posed a critical question to the room:

> "Are you a goal the state wants to support, or a cost to be paid in achieving that goal?"

It may be one of the most important questions to think about in understanding China's economic model — and in investing and living here.

## II. Six Principles of Value Investing

In the same talk, Li Lu returned to value investing and distilled its core into six principles.

### The First Three Come from Benjamin Graham

1. **A stock represents partial ownership of a business**, not merely a piece of paper to trade back and forth. Buying a stock is, in essence, buying a company.
2. **The market is there to serve you, not to instruct you.**
3. **Always demand a margin of safety.** Investing involves predicting the future, and the future is inherently unpredictable. So you should always require a margin of safety — it protects the downside while also improving the upside.

### The Fourth Comes from Warren Buffett

4. **Stay within your "circle of competence."** You don't need to understand every business — only to correctly evaluate the ones inside your circle. The size of the circle doesn't matter; what matters is knowing clearly where its boundary lies.

### The Fifth Comes from Charlie Munger

5. **Fish where the fish are.** Some markets are full of opportunity, where even a mediocre investor can earn decent returns; others are so fiercely competitive that even an excellent investor struggles to do well.

### The Sixth Comes from Li Lu's Own Three Decades of Practice

6. **The essence of wealth is your share (proportion) of global purchasing power — not how much gold, diamonds, or a particular currency you hold.** Under inflation, the purchasing power those things represent erodes over time. The right approach: buy outstanding companies in dynamic countries/markets at favorable prices, hold them for the long term, and let your share of wealth grow as those companies create value.

## Closing Thoughts

The two parts look separate but are two sides of one coin. The first is a read on the **macro environment** — in a system that keeps switching between goals and costs, recognizing which side you are actually on. The second is a summary of **individual methodology** — how, amid uncertainty, to use an ownership mindset, a margin of safety, and a circle of competence to anchor your wealth, over the long run, to companies that genuinely create value.
